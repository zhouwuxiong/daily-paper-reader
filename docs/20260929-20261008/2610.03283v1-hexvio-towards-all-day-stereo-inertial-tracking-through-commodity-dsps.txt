# HexVIO: Towards All-Day Stereo-Inertial Tracking Through Commodity DSPs 

Patrick Wolf ∗1, Mateo de Mayo ∗1,2, Daniel Cremers 1,2

Abstract — The ability of a device to localize itself within its surroundings is a fundamental prerequisite for spatial computing. Visual-inertial odometry (VIO) has proven to be a cost-effective and accurate solution for this task. Robots, wearables, XR devices, and drones can benefit significantly from efficient implementations of VIO since they allow for cooler, lighter, and cheaper devices with longer battery life and a better user experience. In this work, we propose to enhance the efficiency of a VIO system by leveraging the Hexagon DSP, a commodity co-processor present in many modern smartphones and XR devices. Our approach offloads the visual frontend of a stereo-inertial odometry system to the DSP while keeping the backend on the main CPU. By optimizing the implementation for the DSP architecture, we achieve significant reductions in power consumption and latency compared to CPU-only execution. Our system, HexVIO, demonstrates a 67% reduction in power consumption or an 86% increase in throughput on a commodity smartphone, with the ability to sustain long-term real-time 30 fps tracking for 0.83 W, corresponding to ∼18 hours of tracking on the testing device. These results highlight the potential of commodity DSPs for enabling all-day visual-inertial tracking in robotics and mobile devices. 

I. INTRODUCTION Recent advancements in robotics [1], [2], computer vision [3]–[6], and foundation models capable of complex reasoning [7]–[9] have started to show the possibilities of perception engineering [10] as a new discipline and of spatial computing [11] as the ever more common class of problems that require a computing device to understand its surroundings. Different instances of this problem arise in important applications like autonomous vehicles, drones, humanoid robotics, wearables like smartglasses, and VR headsets. A fundamental capability that allows much of this reasoning comes from the ability of the device to localize itself and its movements in space. Utilizing cameras and inertial measurement units (IMU) for 

visual-inertial odometry (VIO) and visual-inertial simultane-ous localization and mapping (VI-SLAM) has shown to be a particularly good combination thanks to the low cost of these sensors, their reduced power consumption, and proven accuracy [12], [13]. The field of VIO and VI-SLAM has made rapid progress in the previous decade enabling this technology to be de-ployed in commercial devices like VR headsets and smart-phones. However, existing limitations in terms of accuracy per watt remain in the literature [14], driving research into

We instead use a co-processor that existing devices already carry. It is nowadays common to find systems, even low-end ones, that have a GPU alongside the CPU. DSPs have developed from fixed-function audio and modem processors into increasingly general-purpose compute units that run at a fraction of the energy cost of a CPU [17]. The Hexagon DSP is now present in a multitude of devices spanning smartphones, VR, AR, and robotics (e.g., Snapdragon 8, XR2, AR1, and RB5 respectively). The DSP provides very long instruction words with wide vector lanes coupled with fast local memory. When properly utilized, this results in significantly reduced power usage for certain tasks compared to using the CPU or GPU. This kind of co-processor allows robots, XR devices, and smartphones to do long-haul vision tasks thanks to the low power required to perform them on these accelerators. The low power not only extends operation, but can also directly improve the target application: smaller batteries for lighter and cheaper drones, lighter headworn devices for reduced neck strain, and cooler smartglasses for improved comfort. The goal we work towards is tracking that runs for a full day on a device’s own battery. Even with all these benefits, very few works in the literature exploit it for the fundamental task of visual-inertial tracking and, to the best of our knowledge, none of them for the stereo-inertial sensor modality. In this work, we offload the frontend of a visual-inertial odometry system based on Basalt [18] to the Hexagon DSP of a Samsung Galaxy S23 (Snapdragon 8 Gen 2, Hexagon V73). A direct port does not beat the CPU. Sparse tracking branches per feature and reads scattered pixels, the vector unit runs all lanes together and its gather reads only on-chip memory, and each DSP call costs hundreds of microseconds, so calling the DSP once per stage spends much of the gain on round trips. RELATED WORK Contemporary VIO and VI-SLAM pipelines generally decompose into a visual frontend, responsible for key-point detection and feature tracking, an inertial integration stage, and a backend optimizer or filter tasked with fusing these modalities. A diverse set of representative systems has emerged in the literature, including optimization-based frameworks such as ORB-SLAM3 [13], which augments feature-based pipelines with inertial measurements and map atlases, and VINS-Mono/VINS-Fusion [19] or OKVIS [20], which rely on sliding-window nonlinear optimization. Filter-based approaches, notably ROVIO [21] and MSCKF [22], also remain widely adopted. In this work, we build upon Basalt [18], incorporating modifications from [23]. Basalt utilizes a fixed-lag smoother that couples patch-based Lucas-Kanade tracking [24], [25], implemented in its inverse-compositional form [26] to keep reference-side linear alge-bra out of the iteration loop, with FAST corner detection [27], preintegrated IMU factors, and first-estimate-Jacobian marginalization. The open implementation of Basalt, along with its balance between performance and accuracy [23], makes it a particularly strong target for frontend acceleration on embedded platforms. 

Mobile VIO systems must additionally sustain the camera frame rate within a strict thermal and power envelope, a dual constraint formalized in embedded VIO benchmarking practices that report tracking accuracy alongside per-frame computational cost on target hardware [28]. One established strategy to meet these constraints involves redesigning the es-timator itself. For instance, LEVIO [16] achieves 20 fps six-DoF tracking below 100 mW on an ultra-low-power RISC-V SoC by employing a streamlined ORB-based frontend alongside a comprehensive hardware-software co-design. In contrast, this work preserves Basalt’s complete pipeline and instead focuses on accelerating its execution. This is areasonable approach given these SoCs are already widely adopted by different industries trying to leverage spatial computing. The visual frontend is a natural hardware acceleration 

target, as its per-feature computations are easy to parallelize and its backend interface reduces to sparse correspondences. Prior work has largely targeted GPUs: vilib [29] and Jetson-SLAM [14] accelerate FAST-based frontends in CUDA (KLT as in ours, and ORB, respectively), while FastTrack [30] offloads portions of ORB-SLAM3 to the GPU. GLidE-SLAM [31] employs OpenGL compute shaders to circum-vent CUDA lock-in and augments indirect features with direct GPU tracking. In contrast, our DSP-based approach frees the GPU for concurrent workloads and sidesteps the thermal constraints and higher power draw typical of GPU acceleration, while still delivering low-power, high-frame-rate tracking. At the other extreme, custom silicon achieves the lowest reported energy per frame. Navion [15] integrates a complete VIO pipeline into a 2 mW ASIC via algorithm-hardware co-design [32], albeit at the cost of fixing the algorithm. FPGA-based solutions offer a more flexible alternative: eSLAM [33] accelerates only ORB extraction and matching, while ac2SLAM [34] accelerates both the ORB-SLAM2 frontend and backend, though neither incorporates inertial fusion. PX4Flow [35] instead computes block-matching optical flow on a microcontroller inside the sensor. Compared to the Hexagon DSP, however, these custom accelerators lack broad availability on commodity platforms, and ASICs offer no post-deployment reprogrammability. 

Hexagon DSPs occupy a middle ground: they are pro-grammable, widely deployed, and optimized for energy-efficient single-instruction multiple-data (SIMD) vector pro-cessing. The Hexagon architecture [17] combines a VLIW scalar core with the HVX wide-vector coprocessor and a software-managed scratchpad. Originally designed for audio and modem tasks, it now supports general-purpose work-loads, including LLM inference [36], while vendor CV/ML stacks (FastCV, SNPE, QNN [37]) and the Halide compiler [38] already target it for dense image processing. Despite this ecosystem, academic use of Hexagon for visual-inertial tracking remains limited. The closest prior work is Muffang’s thesis [39], which offloaded OpenVINS [40] frontend rou-tines to a Snapdragon 845 via FastCV; it reduced CPU load but the offloaded kernels ran slower than their CPU equiv-alents, and no source code was released. An open Hexagon port of the ORB-SLAM3 feature extractor and matcher exists for the Lynx R1 headset [41], but it lacks inertial fusion and requires a rooted device. Crucially, none of the prior efforts support stereo-inertial tracking, a ubiquitous modality in consumer XR and robotics. Relative to this body of work, our system is, to the best of our knowledge, the first to accelerate a multicamera VIO pipeline on the Hexagon DSP of a stock consumer smartphone. While LEVIO shares our energy efficiency goals, it fundamentally redesigns the estimator for minimal compute budgets sacrificing accuracy; approaches like vilib, FastTrack, Jetson-SLAM, and GLidE-SLAM target frontend acceleration but rely on higher-power GPUs; ASIC-, FPGA-and MCU-based solutions like Navion, eSLAM, ac2SLAM, and PX4Flow achieve high efficiency but require hardware that is unavailable on common platforms. Prior Hexagon-based tracking efforts either lack stereo-inertial support, remain closed-source, and/or require rooted devices. In con-trast, our implementation is open-source, runs on unmodified consumer hardware, and demonstrates that the Hexagon DSP can effectively accelerate stereo-inertial VIO frontends at significantly lower power. Memory subsystem: The CPU invokes code on the DSP through FastRPC, a remote-procedure-call layer [42]. On the DSP side, HVX bypasses the scalar core’s L1 and reads from L2 or from VTCM (vector tightly coupled memory), an 8 MB scratchpad under software control. Alongside ordinary loads and stores, DMA can move bulk data from DDR into VTCM. The vector gather instruction can only address VTCM [43, Sec. 3.3], so anything sampled at non-contiguous memory addresses must be resident there rather than merely cached.

One call per stage and camera, plus one for stereo matching, would mean seven calls per frame; fusing them amortizes the fixed cost over the whole frontend (Fig. 6). Apart from the images, the call’s inputs and outputs are sparse feature data that scale with the feature count rather than the image size. The image pyramids must reside in VTCM for the tracking gathers, so the call begins by fetching the full-resolution stereo pair from the shared buffer by DMA and builds the coarser levels on the DSP. A coarse occupancy grid links the stages. Surviving temporal tracks prefill it, so detection searches only cells that still need features; the new left-camera detections then seed stereo tracking, whose matches likewise occupy cells before right-camera detection. Right-camera detection also skips cells that project into the left camera’s view, as in [23]. The frontend uses two vectorization strategies (Fig. 3). Pyramid construction and detection are pixel-parallel. Neigh-boring pixels are contiguous in memory, so one vector load brings in 64 16-bit pyramid pixels or 128 8-bit detector pixels, one per lane, and each lane computes its pixel’s part of the stencil or ring test. Tracking is feature-parallel. Each feature has a KLT patch of 52 sample points spread around it, so a lane holds one feature instead. The 32 features of a batch move through the 52 points in lockstep, and at each point a gather fetches the pixels each feature needs to interpolate it.

D. Detection 

We detect FAST-9 corners [27]. A pixel p is a corner if nine contiguous pixels on the radius-3 Bresenham circle are all brighter than I(p) + τ or all darker than I(p) − τ ,and its score is the largest τ that satisfies this condition. Basalt keeps at most one corner per 50 × 50 cell by running thresholds ⟨40 , 20 , 10 , 5⟩ from strict to lenient and taking the highest-response candidate at the first threshold that yields one. Since the response is the largest passing threshold, that procedure returns the highest-scoring corner in the cell above the minimum threshold. We therefore make one full-image pass at the lowest threshold, τ = 5 , computing the corner test and the score over the ring, and keep the per-cell maximum after non-maximum suppression, obtaining in one vectorized sweep the same corners as Basalt. RESULTS We run all of our experiments on a Samsung Galaxy S23 

with a Snapdragon 8 Gen 2 SoC and a Hexagon V73 DSP. The device is powered by a 15 Wh battery, and we disable cellular data and Wi-Fi to minimize background activity. The screen is dimmed to reduce power consumption further. We use the same camera calibration and configuration within each dataset family to ensure consistency across runs. For the dataset selection we choose sequences spanning different sensors, environments, and motions. We evaluate on eight sequences from four distinct datasets: EuRoC [44] (an MAV), the Monado SLAM Dataset [23] (VR headsets), TUM-VI [45] (handheld rig), and a custom dataset recorded outdoors with a pair of XREAL AIR 2 Ultra glasses (wear-able). We prefix them with E, M, T, and X respectively. Frames from these sequences and their corresponding sensor specifications are shown in the bottom of Fig. 1. The accuracy of our baseline system, Basalt, is benchmarked in [18], [23]. We implement the same algorithm and, as Table I shows, reproduce its accuracy, so we do not repeat that evaluation here. We compare the CPU baseline, the unmodified Basalt fron-tend built with -O3 for the Cortex-A715 and using OpenCV’s and Eigen’s NEON paths, against three DSP configurations, which we denote as DSP , DSP B , and DSP L. For all of them, we pin CPU threads to the mid SoC cluster, which we found to be the most energy-efficient for our workload. DSP is configured for maximum performance, with cDSP running at its TURBO DCVS (Dynamic Clock and Voltage Scaling) corner at 1401.6 MHz, using a polling FastRPC quality-of-service (QoS) policy, and a 40 μs DSP sleep-latency vote to keep the cDSP out of deep idle between calls. Under polling QoS the CPU spins until the call returns; under the adaptive policy it sleeps and is woken at the driver’s predicted completion time. DSP B is configured for maximum efficiency while maintaining the baseline’s accuracy, with the cDSP running at the SVS2 (Static Voltage Scaling) corner at 576 MHz, using an adaptive FastRPC QoS policy, and the default sleep latency. DSP L is similar to DSP B , but we allow a decrease in accuracy by raising the FAST detection threshold floor from 5 to 20 and using 6 keyframes instead of 7 for the optimization. We use Perfetto to record system traces for every run and get the timing, power, clock, and temperature numbers. Table II uses the board thermistor adjacent to the SoC (xo-therm ), while Fig. 7 reports the SoC die temperature, the hottest on-die TSENS zone. Efficiency scaling: Fig. 1 shows the trade-off between power and throughput for both implementations on MOO05. Both curves start at the paced 30 fps configuration and then run unpaced, so that each configuration reaches its maximum frame rate. The DSP curve dominates the CPU curve over its whole span: at the baseline’s best throughput of 98 fps (frontend pinned to the mid cluster, 4.8 W) HexVIO needs 1.6 W, and with only two host threads it already delivers 93 fps at 1.5 W. On MOO05, at the paced sensor rate, where both implementations meet the 30 fps deadline, HexVIO draws 39 % less whole-device power. At the other end, the maximum-performance RPC policy lifts HexVIO to 180 fps at 4.5 W, a power level at which the CPU baseline delivers 97 fps whether pinned or not: left to the scheduler it spends 7.0 W for the same 97 fps, because the prime and little cores join in. Pinning the CPU side of the pipeline to the mid cluster is therefore part of the design, not a benchmark artifact. On the DSP side the polling policy buys its throughput at a steep power price (Table II), which is why DSP B and DSP L keep the driver defaults. 

Real-time behavior: The clearest real-time result is in the late-frame columns of Table II (bottom). Summed over the eight sequences the CPU baseline misses 342 deadlines, DSP B misses 21, and DSP L misses one. For the XREAL sequences, the larger numbers of late frames for the CPU baseline are not a throughput deficit: on frames that arrive on schedule, the CPU baseline misses only 0.16 %. The majority of late frames follow gaps in delivery, after which the governor lowers the mid-cluster clock. On XWALK, 81 % of late frames arrive after a gap, at a mean clock of 0.8 GHz against 1.9 GHz on schedule. The DSP frontend runs at a corner set from user space, so only the backend’s end-to-end time is impacted by the host clock.

V. CONCLUSIONS In this work, we ported the frontend of an open-source stereo VIO system to run on the DSP of an unmodified consumer phone, without root access and without changing the backend estimator. On a 40-minute sequence, we demon-strate that our tracking solution meets its frame deadline at 0.83 W, which corresponds to about 18 hours on a 15 Wh battery, while holding a flat mid-cluster clock and a 34– 35 °C SoC die temperature throughout, against 42 °C for the CPU. Accuracy is preserved against the CPU reference; we reimplement the same algorithm. Fusing the per-stage dispatches into a single call per frame is what makes the efficiency operating points practical: with one call per frame instead of one per stage, the frontend can keep the power-saving RPC policy and still meet the deadline. Future work can start with live sensor data. Qualcomm SoCs can stream the camera output directly into DSP L2 [47], which would remove the DDR round trip for level 0. The interface sits inside the ISP pipeline and is available only to signed vendor code, so this is an OEM integration rather than a drop-in change. The backend optimizer is a second target, since it now accounts for most of the remaining frame time. Finally, Hexagon is not confined to smartphones: it ships in robotics boards such as the RB5 kit, in VR and AR headsets (XR2, AR1), and in automotive platforms (Snapdragon Ride), so a frontend that fits inside a phone’s thermal budget carries over to the devices that spatial computing targets.
