# VGGT-Bridge: Beyond Sequential Pose Graphs via Coarse-Stride Skip Edges 

Sungjae Choi ⋆, Hanna Bae ⋆, Sunghyun Baek, and Junmo Kim 

Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology, South Korea 

{sungjae579, hannabae, baeksh, junmo.kim}@kaist.ac.kr 

Abstract. Feed-forward visual geometry transformers such as VGGT reconstruct dense 3D structure from images in a single forward pass, simplifying multi-view 3D reconstruction. However, their quadratic at-tention complexity makes them difficult to scale to long sequences with thousands of frames. Chunk-and-align frameworks address this by split-ting a long sequence into overlapping chunks and stitching their local reconstructions into a pose graph. Yet existing methods connect only se-quentially adjacent chunks, so small per-frame errors accumulate along the chain into large-scale drift. To move beyond sequential edges, we propose VGGT-Bridge, which adds long-range skip edges that directly constrain non-adjacent chunks without retraining. By running VGGT on sparsely sampled coarse chunks, each coarse chunk bridges distant fine chunks into a single direct constraint. We further turn VGGT’s first-frame scale bias into a drift correction by feeding selected coarse chunks in reverse, and a loop-aware policy keeps this reversal compatible with existing loop closures. VGGT-Bridge reduces ATE by 28.3% on KITTI Odometry, 18.8% on Virtual KITTI, and 10.0% on Waymo Open over the SwiftVGGT baseline, achieving the best performance among all chunk-and-align methods. 

Keywords: Visual Geometry Transformer · Pose Graph Optimization · Long-Sequence SLAM 

## 1 Introduction 

Feed-forward visual geometry transformers such as VGGT [43], DUSt3R [46], and MASt3R [21] predict dense per-frame depth, camera pose and 3D structure directly from a sequence of images in a single forward pass. They have dra-matically simplified multi-view 3D reconstruction by eliminating feature match-ing, bundle adjustment, and iterative refinement. Many real-world applications, such as autonomous driving and large-scale mapping, require reconstructing long video sequences with thousands of frames, yet these models still struggle to scale because their attention complexity grows quadratically with the number of input frames. To scale these models to long sequences, a common chunk-and-align pipeline [8, 20] splits a long sequence into overlapping chunks, runs the

However, these methods connect only temporally adjacent chunks, so two chunks more than one step apart are related solely through the chain of inter-mediate alignments. Small errors in these local alignments propagate along the chain, and even after global pose graph optimization they accumulate into large-scale drift. Also, VGGT anchors each chunk to its first frame as a reference and reconstructs later frames at a progressively smaller scale. This anchoring bias worsens the drift, since the shrinkage acts in one direction and its errors accu-mulate rather than cancel. When a reliable revisit is detected, loop closure can supply occasional non-local constraints, yet such edges are sparse and may be absent in non-revisiting sequences, such as long, loop-free highway driving. Motivated by this, we propose VGGT-Bridge, which adds training-free long-range constraints to the same lightweight pose graph, without retraining the model or using external modules. VGGT-Bridge consists of three components. First, we augment the sequential chain with long-range skip edges between non-adjacent chunks. Running the same model on coarse chunks, which sample frames more sparsely and span a wider temporal range, each coarse chunk bridges and directly constrains the distant fine chunks it overlaps. Second, to correct the anchoring bias, we reverse the input frame order of selected coarse chunks, which shifts their scale against the bias and turns the skip edge into a corrective anti-bias signal for the constructed pose graph. Finally, this reversal can conflict with the loop closures that already correct scale drift in loop-rich regions. We therefore introduce a policy that reverses only where drift dominates and stays forward where reliable loops are present. Beyond the overall accuracy, we directly verify the under-lying mechanism, showing that reverse anchoring shifts the scale of skip edges in the direction opposite to the bias and that this anti-bias signal suppresses global scale drift. Furthermore, we provide component ablations and an efficiency anal-ysis that support the contribution of each design choice. In summary, our key contributions are as follows: 

• We propose VGGT-Bridge, which links non-adjacent chunks with skip edges to add long-range constraints to the chunk-and-align pipeline, without any training or external module. 

• We correct the first-frame anchoring bias of feed-forward models by reversing the input order of selected coarse chunks, with a loop-aware policy setting each chunk’s direction to stay compatible with real loop closures. 

• Through extensive experiments on KITTI Odometry, Waymo Open Dataset, and Virtual KITTI, we show that VGGT-Bridge achieves the best perfor-mance among all chunk-and-align methods, and validate its design through analyses of the scale bias and component ablations. 

## 2 Related Work 

Feed-forward visual geometry models. Classical 3D reconstruction relies on modular pipelines for correspondence estimation, camera recovery, triangulation, and bundle adjustment [1, 15, 23, 31–33]. Learning-based SfM methods improve or differentiate parts of this pipeline [36,38,39,44,48], but still retain explicit ge-ometric estimation and optimization. Feed-forward visual geometry models take a more direct route, predicting dense 3D structure from images with pretrained networks. Beginning with pairwise point map prediction [21, 46], recent systems extend this paradigm with spatial memory, unconstrained SfM, or multi-view reconstruction [9,42,43,47,52]. While these models provide strong short-window geometry, their global attention scales quadratically with the number of frames, making long sequences prohibitively expensive. 

Classical long-scene reconstruction. Long-scene reconstruction has tra-ditionally been addressed by simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) and incremental structure-from-motion (SfM), where a visual front-end establishes short-range geometric constraints from image measurements and a geometric back-end refines camera poses and map structure over time [3, 10, 11, 17, 27, 28, 34,39,40]. These systems combine local tracking or odometry with map-level op-timization, and reduce long-term drift by adding non-local constraints from loop detection and visual place recognition [16, 30]. The resulting loop edges are in-corporated through pose graph or factor graph optimization backends [7,14,18]. This pipeline has been highly effective for long sequences, but it relies on a carefully designed stack of correspondence estimation, mapping, place recogni-tion, and optimization. Recent feed-forward approaches pursue the same goal 4 S. Choi et al. 

of long-scene consistency while replacing much of this hand-designed front-end with learned dense geometry priors. 

Extending feed-forward models to long scenes. One line of work mod-ifies the model or inference process itself, using spatial memory, recurrent states, streaming formulations, or test-time training to extend the temporal context available to the network [4–6,19,45,49,50,54,55]. These approaches broaden the usable context, but often require retraining, architectural changes, or a stream-ing reformulation. A complementary direction keeps the pretrained model fixed and moves long-scene consistency outside the network by constructing a graph over local reconstructions [24, 25, 29, 53]. Chunk-and-align pipelines follow this route. They split a long sequence into overlapping chunks, run the feed-forward model on each, and fuse the local reconstructions in a global pose graph. VGGT-Long [8] aligns neighboring chunks with Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) over Sim(3) and adds loop closures from visual place recognition, while SwiftVGGT [20] replaces the iterative alignment with a single-shot Umeyama SVD [41] step and reuses the model’s internal descriptors for loop detection. VGGT-Motion [51] uses motion-aware submaps to reduce redundant VGGT processing and improve submap alignment. Despite these differences, existing graph-based approaches remain largely sequential, with most constraints com-ing from adjacent chunk overlaps and non-local edges mainly arising from loop closures. Our work instead introduces additional long-range constraints that con-nect distant chunks beyond the sequential overlap chain. 

## 3 Method 

3.1 Overview 

We first review the chunk-and-align pipeline that our method builds on, which reconstructs a long sequence by splitting it into chunks and stitching their local reconstructions into a single pose graph. We then give an overview of VGGT-Bridge, which augments this pose graph with training-free long-range edges to reduce the drift that accumulates along the sequence.

3.3 Reverse-anchored coarse chunks 

VGGT anchors each chunk at its first input frame, and this anchoring biases the reconstructed scale to shrink away from that anchor as the input advances. Since every chunk is anchored the same way, this bias always acts in the same direction, so the scale drift accumulates without canceling along the sequential edges. A loop closure would correct this drift by supplying the pose graph with a constraint from a revisited place, but such a constraint is available only where a revisit is detected, leaving the drift uncorrected in loop-free regions. In these re-gions, we repurpose the skip edge as a pseudo loop closure, a non-local constraint that corrects drift in place of a real loop closure. A default skip edge is built from coarse chunks that are anchored at their first frame, just like the fine chunks, so it carries the same first-frame bias and cannot counteract the drift it shares with the sequential edges. We reverse this bias by feeding the frames of the coarse chunk that forms the skip edge in reverse temporal order. The forward-anchored chunk Cr, fwd  

> k

, used everywhere so far, feeds its frames (f1, . . . , f Br ) in temporal order and is anchored at the earliest frame f1. The reverse-anchored chunk Cr, rev

feeds the same frames in reverse order (fBr , . . . , f 1), moving the anchor to the latest frame fBr , and its outputs are then reordered to temporal order for alignment. We call the skip edge composed from a forward-anchored chunk a forward skip edge, and the one composed from a reverse-anchored chunk a reverse skip edge. Reversing the input leaves the composition of Eq. (3) unchanged but moves the anchor from the first frame to the last, so the scale bias now acts in the opposite direction. The reverse skip edge therefore introduces a scale constraint in the direction opposite to the accumulated forward drift, acting as an anti-bias constraint. 

3.4 Loop-aware adaptive direction policy 

The reversal above targets loop-free regions, but a sequence also contains loop-rich ones, where the loop edges in loop edge set Eloop already constrain the drift. There, a reverse skip edge would pull against these loop edges, so we keep loop-rich regions forward and reverse only where loops are absent. Assigning a single direction to all coarse chunks of each stride cannot adapt to both region types, so we choose the direction per coarse chunk from the local loop density. 5.2 Scale Bias Analysis and Ablations 

We analyze VGGT’s scale bias at two stages of the pipeline and confirm that reverse anchoring corrects it as intended. We estimate the scale bias in chunk alignments and the residual scale error against the ground-truth, shown respec-tively in the left and right panels of Fig. 4. 

The bias exists and shifts with anchoring. We record the pairwise Umeyama scale s of every alignment that forms an edge in the pose graph on KITTI Odome-try 00–10, and report its mean and standard deviation over the eleven sequences. Fine-sequential alignments are always forward-anchored, whereas each coarse chunk is measured both forward-anchored and reverse-anchored. ## 6 Conclusion 

We present VGGT-Bridge, a training-free method that adds long-range skip edges to the chunk-and-align 3D reconstruction pipeline. By running VGGT at coarse strides, each coarse chunk bridges non-adjacent fine chunks into a direct constraint. Reverse anchoring then turns VGGT’s first-frame scale bias into a drift correction, while a loop-aware policy keeps this reversal compatible with real loop closures. Across KITTI Odometry, Waymo Open Dataset, and Virtual KITTI, VGGT-Bridge attains the best mean ATE RMSE among all chunk-and-align methods, reducing the error by 28.3%, 10.0%, and 18.8% over the SwiftVGGT baseline. Our mechanism analysis confirms that reverse anchoring counteracts the scale bias of skip edges and suppresses the accumulated scale drift, and our ablations show that each component contributes to the final per-formance. These results show that adding long-range skip edges is a practical and effective way to reduce accumulated drift in chunk-and-align reconstruction.

## C Cross-framework transfer: VGGT-Long 

To test whether our contribution is tied to a particular alignment style or is a general pose-graph augmentation, we transplant the full VGGT-Bridge recipe onto VGGT-Long [8]. The two backbones differ only in how each chunk align-ment Sim(3) is estimated, while sharing the same Sim(3) pose graph and the same Levenberg–Marquardt [22,26] solver. SwiftVGGT estimates it with a single Umeyama step on the filtered 3D points, as in our default setting. VGGT-Long instead runs Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS), repeating a weighted Umeyama step while recomputing the Huber term of each weight from its cur-rent residual. Each weight combines the VGGT confidence with a Huber term that downweights high-residual points, so unreliable ones contribute less as the iterations proceed. The two estimators differ sharply in this respect, so transfer-ring the same method across them tests whether the skip edge is specific to the single-step Umeyama estimator or generalizes to an iteratively reweighted one. The three method components transfer as is, namely the coarse-stride skip edge, the reverse-anchored coarse chunk, and the loop-aware adaptive direction policy. The only change is inside skip-edge composition, where each fine–coarse 

Sim(3) is now solved by the confidence-weighted IRLS estimator rather than a VGGT-Bridge: Coarse-Stride Skip Edges 21 

single Umeyama step, matching the host backbone. In Eq. (7), the edge type with the larger alignment residual can dominate the optimization, so we set the skip-edge scalars from the ratio of skip-edge to sequential-edge residuals. On SwiftVGGT, the median Umeyama residual of a skip edge is 0.106 against 

0.052 for a sequential edge, a ratio of 2.0×. IRLS on VGGT-Long tightens the sequential edges to 0.024 while the skip edges stay at 0.123 , raising the ratio to 5.1×. We therefore recalibrate three scalars so the looser skip constraint is weighted appropriately against the tighter sequential and loop edges. We tighten the Huber threshold to δ=0 .05 , so the IRLS reweighting discounts high-residual correspondences more aggressively. We lower the skip-edge base weight to ¯w=0 .1,so that the looser skip edges do not dominate the tighter sequential edges. We set the loop-aware adaptive direction threshold to τ =0 .05 , so reverse anchoring is applied only where loops are essentially absent. ## D Chamfer Evaluation

## F Per-component runtime breakdown and memory 

To isolate where the 1.5× slowdown in Table 6 of the main paper comes from, we measure per-stage wall-clock time on a short and a long sequence, KITTI Odometry Seq. 07 (1,101 frames) and Seq. 08 (4,071 frames). While Table 6 times the full pipeline, here we report only the major computation stages, so that the effect of adding VGGT-Bridge is visible per stage. Table 13 reports the breakdown together with the coarse chunk and skip edge counts that drive each stage. The added cost is concentrated in the coarse VGGT [43] inference and the skip-edge composition. These two stages account for nearly all of the overhead, adding +26 .3 s and +28 .5 s on Seq. 07 and +110 .3 s and +38 .7 s on Seq. 08. Each coarse chunk spans far fewer frames than a fine chunk, with B3=35 and B5=25 

against the 75 frames of a fine chunk. The extra VGGT inference therefore stays a moderate trade-off, despite the larger number of coarse chunks. The pose graph optimization absorbs the additional skip edges with little overhead, since skip edges add only constraints and the number of pose variables to solve stays the same. ## G Compute-matched comparison 

Our method feeds more frames to VGGT than SwiftVGGT, since it runs the coarse chunks in addition to the fine chunks. To test whether the gain comes from this extra computation alone, we add computation to the default SwiftVGGT setting ( B=75 , O=30 ) in two ways, as reported in Table 15. The first raises the overlap O, giving each sequential alignment more shared frames. The second runs an additional tiling with chunk size B′=90 and jointly optimizes both tilings in one pose graph. Both variants feed VGGT as many frames as our method or more, yet both worsen the mean ATE of SwiftVGGT, while our method lowers it to 22 .61 . The gain therefore comes from our design rather than from the extra computation.

Coarse stride set. Dropping either coarse stride degrades the mean, and stride 3 is the more important of the two. Removing stride 3 is the most damaging change at +4 .73 m, while removing stride 5 still costs +1 .91 m, so both strides contribute and we keep them. Adding stride 7 on top of the default slightly hurts the mean ( +0 .29 m). ## J Limitations 

Our framework inherits limitations from both the VGGT backbone and the loop-closure detector. Because it operates at the graph level, it does not directly denoise the VGGT predictions themselves. Severe backbone errors can therefore still be propagated through the cross-scale skip edges. The adaptive anchoring policy is also limited by the reliability of loop detection, and missed loop closures in visually ambiguous or textureless scenes can lead to suboptimal anchor se-lection. VGGT-Bridge requires additional VGGT runs on coarse-stride chunks, increasing inference time. Like other VGGT-based reconstruction methods, it as-sumes a predominantly static scene, and handling dynamic objects is orthogonal to our contribution.
