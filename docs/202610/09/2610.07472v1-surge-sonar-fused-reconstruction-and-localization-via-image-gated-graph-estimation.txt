Abstract —Remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) are widely used to explore and inspect underwater environments such as caves, shipwrecks, and submerged infrastructure. These missions require accurate 3D understanding of the surrounding environment, which depends on both reliable vehicle localization and metric scene reconstruction. However, external positioning is often unavailable underwater, requiring small ROVs to rely primarily on onboard perception. Optic vision provides rich visual and geometric information but suffers from scale ambi-guity and trajectory drift, whereas 2D imaging sonar provides metric range but incomplete 3D geometry. Existing underwater reconstruction approaches typically address these limitations separately or assume known sensor poses, leaving localization and reconstruction disconnected. We present SURGE, a camera sonar framework that jointly estimates the ROV trajectory and target location by integrating visual and acoustic observations within a factor graph, then uses the recovered metric poses for sonar Gaussian splatting. Experiments on real underwater RGB sonar observations show that SURGE substantially improves localization consistency over conventional vision based pose estimation and produces a more compact, natively metric reconstruction than RGB Gaussian splatting baselines. 

Index Terms —Underwater perception, sensor fusion, factor graph SLAM, forward-looking sonar, Gaussian splatting, re-motely operated vehicles. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Remotely operated vehicles are increasingly used for un-derwater exploration and inspection of environments such as caves, shipwrecks, and submerged infrastructure [1]–[4]. These missions require accurate three dimensional under-standing of the environment for navigation, inspection, and spatial mapping. Building such representations, however, depends on both reliable vehicle localization and metric scene geometry. Underwater localization is particularly challenging because external positioning is often unavailable, forcing small ROVs to rely on onboard perception [24]. Monocular cameras provide rich visual and geometric information but suffer from 

Fig. 1: A representative scenario of an ROV scanning a cinder-block target, with the imaging sonar’s and camera’s fields of view projected onto the object for localization and reconstruction. scale ambiguity and trajectory drift, especially when visi-bility and feature quality degrade underwater [25]. Forward looking imaging sonar provides complementary metric range measurements and remains effective under poor visibility, but conventional two dimensional sonar observes range and azimuth while leaving elevation ambiguous [30], [31]. Nei-ther modality alone therefore provides both reliable metric localization and complete scene geometry, as shown in Fig. 1. Recent acoustic optical methods such as AONeuS and Z Splat demonstrate the benefit of combining camera and sonar measurements for underwater reconstruction [8], [9]. How-ever, these approaches largely assume known sensor poses, while other methods address pose estimation and reconstruc-tion primarily within a single sensing modality [10], [13], [14]. This leaves a gap between multimodal reconstruction and the localization needed to support it in real underwater arXiv:2610.07472v1 [cs.RO] 5 Oct 2026 operation. This separation is limiting in practice because reconstruc-tion quality depends directly on the accuracy and metric consistency of the estimated sensor trajectory. We present 

SURGE , a camera sonar framework for joint underwater localization and metric reconstruction. Our contributions are threefold: 1) We introduce a multimodal factor graph that jointly estimates the ROV trajectory and the location of a compact underwater target from monocular RGB and two dimensional forward looking imaging sonar obser-vations. 2) We develop a multi view sonar aggregation strategy and a target extent aware acoustic residual that pro-vide stable metric constraints for joint pose and target estimation. 3) We use the recovered metric poses for target-scoped sonar Gaussian splatting and demonstrate improved localization consistency and more compact metric re-construction than RGB Gaussian-splatting baselines on real underwater data. II. R ELATED WORK 

Recent underwater reconstruction methods differ primarily in two aspects: the sensing modalities they use and whether sensor poses are known or must be estimated from the observations. These distinctions are important for SURGE because accurate reconstruction depends on both complemen-tary visual acoustic geometry and a reliable metric trajectory. Table I summarizes representative approaches and the setting considered in this work. 

A. Multi-modal Reconstruction with Known Poses 

Camera and sonar provide complementary information for underwater reconstruction, motivating methods that combine optical appearance with acoustic geometry. AONeuS [8] and Z-Splat [9] demonstrate that acoustic measurements can improve three dimensional reconstruction when optical observations are degraded by underwater visibility. Collado-Gonzalez et al. similarly combine image regions with sonar range measurements for reconstruction in turbid environ-ments [26]. These methods establish the value of acoustic optical fusion, but primarily assume externally provided sensor poses. As a result, they address reconstruction once the trajectory is known rather than recovering localization and reconstruction from the same measurements. 

B. Single-Modality Reconstruction with Estimated Poses 

A parallel line of work relaxes the requirement for known poses, but generally within a single sensing modality. Sonar-Splat [10] extends Gaussian splatting to forward-looking sonar while jointly optimizing the acoustic scene representa-tion and sensor poses. Differentiable space carving recon-structs occupancy from imaging-sonar observations when poses are available [37], while other approaches address reconstruction under pose drift [11] or recover sonar ge-ometry without supplied poses using priors from vision foundation models [12]. Together, these methods show that acoustic observations can support both pose reasoning and reconstruction despite the geometric ambiguity of imaging sonar. For optical sensing, SeaSplat [13] and WaterSplatting [14] reconstruct real underwater scenes from RGB imagery using COLMAP poses, while AquaSplat [17] introduces a dual-branch representation for underwater RGB reconstruction. These methods improve the representation of underwater appearance and geometry, but their reconstruction remains tied to trajectories recovered from visual Structure-from-Motion. Thus, pose-aware reconstruction has been explored for both optical and acoustic sensing, but primarily without using their complementary measurements jointly to recover a metric trajectory. 

C. Underwater Factor-Graph SLAM 

Underwater SLAM provides the complementary perspec-tive of estimating vehicle motion from multiple sensor con-straints. Existing systems combine acoustic range, inertial sensing, and optical observations to estimate trajectories and map features [15], [16], [35], [36]. Imaging-sonar multi-robot systems further recover trajectories and object-level maps from multibeam measurements [27], [28]. These approaches demonstrate the value of acoustic constraints for underwater localization, but are not designed to connect monocular RGB and 2D FLIS localization directly to downstream target reconstruction. The observation model is also different for compact ex-tended targets. Conventional landmark formulations typi-cally associate acoustic observations with discrete features, whereas the dominant return from an extended object can move across its visible surface as the viewpoint changes. SURGE therefore models the sonar observation relative to the spatial extent of the target rather than forcing every acoustic return to coincide with a single geometric center. 

D. Position of the Present Work 

Prior work therefore establishes three complementary ca-pabilities: acoustic optical fusion can improve underwater re-construction when poses are known, single-modality methods can reconstruct scenes while estimating or tolerating pose uncertainty, and underwater SLAM can recover vehicle tra-jectories from acoustic and visual constraints. What remains less explored is their combination: jointly estimating metric vehicle motion and target location from monocular RGB and 2D forward-looking sonar, then using the recovered trajectory for reconstruction in real underwater environments without external positioning. SURGE addresses this gap by coupling visual and acoustic observations within a factor graph and using the resulting metric poses for target-scoped sonar Gaussian splatting. The framework therefore connects localization and reconstruction through the same complementary camera-sonar measure-ments rather than treating accurate poses as an independent prerequisite. At a high level, SURGE recovers a metric ROV trajectory and a static target centre from paired RGB and 2D sonar frames, then reconstructs the target with sonar Gaussian splatting under those poses. Vision tells where the object appears in the image; sonar tells how far it is. The factor graph fuses both, and the reconstruction stage consumes only the resulting poses. The pipeline is summarised in Fig. 2. Segmentation and SfM are treated as fixed inputs; the method itself has four stages aligned with that figure. Sec. III-A states the sensing configuration, lever arm, and assumed target half-extents h. Sec. III-B builds the acoustic observation: RGB-gated peak candidates on the FLIS fan are aggregated by a multi-view vote into one world-consistent orange flash, which is then reprojected into each frame, matching the feature-extraction and sonar peak estimation path in Fig. 2. Sec. III-C jointly estimates metric camera poses against that flash, a frozen landmark box of extents h, and RGB centroid/box fac-tors inside the factor-graph modelling / factors-optimization block. An important modelling choice in that graph is the polar disk sonar residual: because the dominant FLIS return typically sits on a near face rather than at the geometric cen-tre b, a hard centre-to-peak residual is inconsistent, and the disk tolerates that offset without discarding the measurement. Sec. III-D then runs cone-arch seed collection and 2D-sonar Gaussian splatting on the recovered poses (Fig. 2).

A brightest-bin factor fails under clutter (rock, tether, sidelobe). We build one acoustic observation per frame in three steps (Fig. 4); the same flow appears in the feature-extraction and peak-estimation stages of Fig. 2. Raw sonar peaks are noisy: clutter, tether, and sidelobes can outshine the target. We therefore build one consensus acoustic observation per frame–a range–azimuth measurement that is consistent across views. The polar renderer follows [21]; we do not claim a new splatting algorithm. SURGE contributes estimated metric poses and target-scoped cone-arch seeding on real data. Given metric poses and b, we reconstruct only the target neighbourhood with 2D-sonar Gaussian splatting. Seeding is restricted to short elevation arches near b (cone-arch seeds) so that the cloud stays on the object rather than filling the seafloor. V. D ISCUSSION 

A. What the pipeline recovers 

SURGE recovers metric-scale ROV poses, a static tar-get centre, and a compact sonar Gaussian cloud from a monocular camera and 2D FLIS only (Fig. 2). Median orange residuals are on the order of one range bin, and the reconstruction is natively metric and much closer to the localised landmark than RGB splatting on COLMAP poses. The cloud is intentionally target-scoped: it is not a face-accurate brick and not a full-scene map. Vertical structure is limited by FLIS angular resolution, the unmeasured ±25 ◦

elevation aperture, and a mostly horizontal orbit. 

B. Why the disk residual matters 

A hard centre-to-peak sonar residual is inconsistent for an extended target: the dominant return usually sits on a near face, not at b. An earlier nearest-face model with Huber weighting looked correct but silently down-weighted informative frames and collapsed range by about a metre. Zeroing cost inside a polar disk of radius d=12 cm (on the order of the assumed half-extents) keeps those observations active. When a measurement is useful but ambiguous within a modelling tolerance, that tolerance belongs in the residual geometry, not only in a robust weight. 

C. Limits of open-water reconstruction 

Denser or sharper geometry is hard to justify from this bag. The orbit has little elevation diversity; FLIS provides no elevation measurement; and the target occupies only a small fraction of each fan. RGB can look detailed in image space yet miss metric scale, while sonar alone leaves a vertical slab. Under these views and sensors, a compact, correctly located, natively metric cloud near the localised landmark is the strongest reconstruction claim we can support. 

D. Discarded design alternatives 

Several alternatives failed for clear reasons. Peak-as-centre 

cannot go to zero as the return walks over faces, so b chases glints. Freeze COLMAP, optimise only b locks scale error (median |∆r|≈ 1.89 m). Strong absolute COLMAP copies multi-metre teleports. Full-fan seeding produces a seafloor pancake; seeds must stay near b. Sub-bin initial covariance 

removes the polar gradient and collapses opacity within a few epochs. 

E. Scope and future work 

Assumed half-extents h set both the box prior and the disk scale; unknown-size targets would need a jointly estimated extent, which we do not evaluate. The orange consensus assumes one compact static object. Within small-ROV in-spection of such a target with camera and 2D FLIS only, this is the setting we claim. The main remaining limit is viewpoint diversity, not the renderer. Closing the loop with active next-best-view control—motions that reduce uncertainty in the joint pose– landmark estimate and in the worst-constrained directions of the per-Gaussian information matrix—is the natural continu-ation [21], [33], [34]. VI. C ONCLUSION 

We presented SURGE, a camera–sonar factor-graph pipeline that recovers metric ROV poses and a compact target reconstruction without external positioning. RGB gating and multi-view orange consensus stabilise the acoustic mea-surement; a polar disk residual tolerates face-versus-centre geometry under an assumed box prior; and a jump-gated SfM prior absorbs COLMAP teleports without copying them. On a real freshwater orbit, the estimator reaches centimetre-scale orange residuals, and cone-arch sonar Gaussian splatting yields a natively metric cloud near the localised landmark— stronger support than RGB densification on COLMAP poses under the same views.
