# GlassGuard: Verified Glass Plane Mapping for Robot Navigation 

Hanwen Guo 1, Zhengzhi Lin 1, Yusen Xie 1, and Ji Zhang 1

Abstract —Transparent and specular surfaces pose a serious challenge to LiDAR-based SLAM and navigation because laser returns may pass through glass, leaving collision boundaries absent from the map. Prior work attempts to reconstruct the missing surfaces, but inaccurate obstacle placement can create the opposite failure: contamination of traversable free space. Recognizing this dual requirement, we present GlassGuard, a navigation-oriented framework for reconstructing planar archi-tectural glass from complementary visual and LiDAR evidence. We formulate success in terms of both glass coverage and free-space contamination and apply this principle throughout proposal verification and global map construction. A foundation vision model provides glass-instance masks, structural 3D cues generate metric plane hypotheses, and depth-free 2D projective geometry checks their orientations before they enter a consol-idated global map. We evaluate GlassGuard in nine building-scale scenes spanning diverse glass structures, spatial scales, and lighting conditions, with more than one hour and 2.1 km of real-world robot traversal. GlassGuard achieves 85% of total glass coverage for its panoramic version. Under identical pinhole inputs, GlassGuard achieves 82% total coverage, compared with at most 61% for the evaluated baselines, while producing 5– 17 × fewer false voxels per frame. Qualitative examples with a navigation planner illustrate the reconstructed planes blocking paths through glass while leaving traversable routes open. The project page is available at https://glassguardproject.github.io/. 

Index Terms —Mapping, Sensor Fusion, Range Sensing, Vision-Based Navigation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Mobile robots commonly rely on LiDAR-based SLAM for autonomous movement. However, transparent surfaces remain a fundamental challenge for light-based sensing: laser energy may be transmitted through glass, specularly reflected, or only weakly returned, causing ghost points or missed detections. This problem is particularly relevant to widely used compact LiDARs, which operate at 905 nm [1]. Returns from glass are highly variable and unreliable [2], [3], potentially producing incomplete or corrupted maps that compromise scene recon-struction, exploration, and safe navigation. One way to address this is to exploit physical cues such as return intensity, reflection patterns, or additional sonar. Although effective, this route depends on the returns, viewing geometry, and sensor measurements that happen to be observ-able, or on additional hardware. A more broadly accessible alternative is to combine visual glass detection with surround-ing 3D geometry. Current efforts can already localize glass in an image, but converting that detection into a correctly placed 3D surface remains challenging. This step can be accomplished with a

learning-based or a pipeline-based strategy. A learning-based strategy directly infers glass depth or plane parameters from observed RGB, and sometimes depth. However, architectural glass offers almost no features of its own: what a camera sees at a pane is predominantly the scene behind or reflected in it, which makes the mapping from image appearance to metric depth ambiguous. Errors in the predicted depth or orientation can therefore place obstacles in traversable space. A pipeline-based strategy instead anchors image evidence, such as detections or learned depth priors, to nearby metric RGB-D or LiDAR measurements. But, placement requires associating glass with the correct structure: an apparent frame or edge may belong to an occluder, or structure behind the glass (Fig. 1). Both strategies can therefore produce plau-sible but incorrectly placed planes in complex scenes. We therefore introduce GlassGuard, an onboard, VRAM-efficient framework that jointly addresses the metric placement of architectural glass while checking each proposed plane against the evidence obtained from the image. Rather than relying on an end-to-end model to generalize directly to 3D glass geometry, GlassGuard shifts the learned component to the more reliable task of 2D glass detection using the foundation model SAM3. It then constructs metric 3D candidates from structural line features extracted from surrounding LiDAR points, reducing the influence of noisy or spurious returns, and verifies their orientations using depth-free projective geometry before admitting them to the map. Accepted observations are consolidated into persistent world-frame planes that can be reinforced, trimmed, or removed. The resulting map is de-signed to cover physical glass while limiting false occupancy in traversable space. We evaluate GlassGuard on a Mecanum-wheeled robot across nine building-scale scenes, six indoor and outdoor environments, and 2.1 km of robot traversal. Under matched pinhole inputs, GlassGuard achieves higher glass coverage and substantially less false occupancy than the evaluated learning-based and pipeline-based baselines, while its panoramic vari-ant further improves early coverage. II. R ELATED WORK 

Physical-Cue-Based Glass Perception. Early robotic sys-tems addressed laser-scanner failures around mirrors and win-dows or adapted localization to glass-walled environments [4], [5]. Subsequent methods exploit glass-specific physical cues such as return intensity, reflection patterns, anomalous or multipath measurements, and view-dependent reflectance [2], [3], [6], [7]. Several systems further incorporate detected glass or reflective planes into local and global SLAM maps [8], [9], [10]. Other approaches confirm suspected glass through physical contact [11] or fusion of time-of-flight and ultrasonic arXiv:2610.02110v1 [cs.RO] 1 Oct 2026 sensing [12]. These methods depend on observable sensor cues, surface interaction, or additional sensing hardware. Our work considers the complementary setting in which reliable measurement and additional sensing hardware cannot be as-sumed available. 

2D Glass Detection. Visual glass perception has ad-vanced substantially over the past decade. Representative works include monocular mirror and glass segmentation [13], [14], reflection- and semantic-context modeling [15], [16], polarization-based perception [17], [18], and boundary-aware or progressive refinement [19], [20]. More recent meth-ods exploit reciprocal feature evolution, ghosting cues, and foundation-model or diffusion priors [21], [22], [23], [24]. These advances make image-space glass localization increas-ingly reliable, but 2D segmentation alone does not provide the metric 3D location at which a detected surface should be inserted into a navigation map. 

Visual-to-3D Glass Reconstruction. Methods that recon-struct glass from visual input are the closest to our setting and serve as our baselines. GW-Depth is a supervised monocular estimator that combines glass-structure and reflection contexts [25]; MonoGlass3D, which we evaluate as the representative learned baseline, jointly predicts glass masks and plane pa-rameters [26]. As the representative pipeline-based baseline, GlassRecon [27] (Zheng et al. ) aligns a foundation-model depth prior with metric RGB-D measurements using local RANSAC; for brevity we refer to this pipeline as GlassRecon, the name of its released dataset. These methods place each surface from a single frame without checking its orientation or depth, and never revise it afterwards. GlassGuard instead uses 2D image geometry and persistent plane management to verify and update the reconstructed glass. III. P ROBLEM FORMULATION

A. Overview 

GlassGuard organizes detection as three stages in which each module contributes only what it is reliable for (Fig. 2). 

First , a quantized Slim SAM3 student detects glass-instance masks (Sec. IV-B); each mask is associated with the sur-rounding LiDAR geometry to extract seed points (projected 3D points used to localize glass surfaces). Selected seeds are then grouped into vertical and horizontal pillars, from which bounded 3D plane candidates are constructed (Sec. IV-C). Sec-ond , the same mask supplies a depth-free orientation reference against which the metric 3D candidates are checked: single-frame ray-cast verification rejects candidates with inconsistent orientations (Sec. IV-D). Third , a global hypothesis manager merges or absorbs overlapping plane hypotheses using accu-mulated seed support and inserts the consolidated bounded planes into the navigation map as occupied space (Sec. IV-E). This combination preserves glass coverage while limiting the contamination of navigable free space by erroneous planes— the two conditions of (1).

C. Associate 2D to 3D: Pillar Construction 

Fitting planes using raw LiDAR points is unreliable because returns are sparse and often hit the cases in Figure 1. We instead construct candidates from structural line evidence associated with glass frames. Starting from LiDAR seeds associated with the glass mask, we extract two structural primitives. Vertical pillars are cells occupied across several consecutive height layers, while hor-izontal pillars are dense seed clusters with a strong line-like PCA response in the horizontal plane. These structures capture common glass-frame elements and provide more stable evidence than direct fitting to sparse returns. The detected structures are used to form metric plane can-didates. Two vertical pillars determine a candidate direction, whereas a horizontal pillar provides the direction directly from its axis.

E. Global Management 

A plane that appears plausible in one frame may be contra-dicted by later observations. Instead of permanently inserting every frame-level result, we maintain each accepted plane as a global hypothesis that can be reinforced, merged, or removed. The global manager contains three components: plane merging combines repeated estimates of the same pane, seed–floor evidence removes planes contradicted by walkable floor, and multi-view verification removes planes that become inconsistent from new viewpoints. 

Global plane merging. Each frame may produce a new plane estimate for glass that has already been observed. Because these estimates can differ slightly in orientation, po-sition, or extent, inserting all of them directly would gradually fill the map with a cluster of overlapping and misaligned planes. We therefore compare every newly solved plane with the planes already maintained in the global map. To determine whether a new plane and an existing track represent the same glass region, we compare them inside the angular wedge formed by the new plane’s boundary rays. An existing track competes with the new plane when at least 

65% of its ground-plane segment lies inside this wedge, when both boundary rays intersect it, or when one ray intersects it and their ground-segment overlap exceeds 70% of the shorter segment. Each track maintains the total number of supporting LiDAR seeds accumulated over time. This seed count is the primary support score: the better-supported competitor sur-vives and absorbs the extent of the weaker estimate. Vertically separated tracks do not compete because they may represent distinct structures. 

Seed–floor evidence. A false glass plane may cross floor that later observations identify as walkable, but a single floor observation is insufficient because real glass frequently reaches the ground. We therefore maintain competing floor and glass-seed evidence in a 0.1 m ground grid (Fig. 2, Global Seed-Floor Evidence Map ): an observation adds one count to its supported class and subtracts one from the opposing class, with both counts clamped to [0 , 3] . A plane falling on a voxel dominated by floor points is removed, since that voxel should be a walkable region. 

Multi-view verification. A correctly placed plane should remain aligned with the detected glass region when observed from different robot poses. For each re-observation we define the off-mask fraction as the fraction of the plane’s visi-ble image projection lying outside all current glass masks (Fig. 2, Multi-View Reprojection Verification ). Only well-observed tracks are judged. A correctly placed plane still shows a small off-mask fraction because masks under-segment the glass, so each track takes the median of its first two judgments as its own tolerance. An off-mask fraction that later exceeds this tolerance by more than 0.10 and is still rising adds one to an evidence counter, a falling one subtracts one, and the track is evicted when the counter reaches two; an off-mask fraction of at least 0.55 evicts it immediately. This test addresses incorrect-depth planes that can pass the single-view yaw check (Fig. 1(b)): such a plane may align in its original view, but its projection separates from the glass mask as the viewpoint changes. 

Planner integration. Each cycle, the current track set is rendered as dense wall points and inserted into the scans con-sumed by terrain analysis and the planners. Nothing persists inside the planner itself: as the glass map evolves, so does the planner’s input. A placed plane therefore blocks paths even after leaving the field of view, and a removed plane disappears from the collision map on the next cycle (Sec. V). V. E XPERIMENTS 

A. Real-World Setup 

We evaluate GlassGuard and the baselines using aMecanum-wheel robot equipped with a Livox Mid-360 LiDAR operating at 10 Hz [1] and a 1920 ×640 Ricoh Theta panoramic camera. At each frame, every LiDAR-using method receives the same 5 s stack of registered point clouds together with synchronized RGB input. The pinhole methods receive the same pinhole RGB view, while GG-360 receives the full 360 ◦

panoramic image. GlassGuard runs onboard at a mean of approximately 0.74 s per frame on an NVIDIA RTX 5070 Ti laptop GPU. Its reconstructed planes are inserted into a LiDAR autonomy stack that provides state estimation, terrain analysis, local planning, and TARE exploration [32], [33]. We recorded nine scenes across six indoor and outdoor environments under daytime and nighttime conditions, totaling more than one hour and 2.1 km of robot traversal. The scenes contain curtain walls, glass doors, office partitions, frosted panels, glass balustrades, and tinted exterior windows, under sunlight, reflections, and nighttime lighting. Planes are placed within 10 m of the robot, or 20 m in the two large outdoor scenes. For evaluation, each method is replayed indepen-dently on the recorded rosbags using the original timestamps, simulating its onboard execution. The resulting timestamped predictions are then evaluated against the same manually annotated ground truth. 

B. Evaluation Metrics 

We evaluate glass coverage and false occupancy on acommon voxel grid fixed in the shared world frame. 

Warning-distance coverage. Let Dk(v) be the Euclidean distance between the robot and the center of GT glass voxel v

at frame k. Voxel v is detected by distance d if it is occupied in at least one frame where d ≤ Dk(v) ≤ 13 m and is line-of-sight visible. Visibility is determined by ray tracing against observed non-glass surfaces on a 0.2 m grid. GT glass beyond the sensing range is excluded from coverage. Coverage is 

C(d) = {v | v is detected by d}

{v | v is visible at some Dk(v) ≥ d} . (11) For pinhole methods, eligibility additionally requires the voxel to enter the camera frustum while the robot is at least d meters away. Ever coverage reports detection in any eligible frame, regardless of distance. 

Current-map coverage and retention. Current-map cov-erage measures how much nearby glass is represented in the map at each frame. We use the same eligibility rules as (11). A voxel counts as covered if the current map marks it or an adjacent cell as occupied, allowing a one-voxel tolerance. A previous detection counts only while it remains in the map. Thus, both delayed detection and later removal reduce coverage for each affected frame. We report coverage separately for the collision-critical 0–

2 m and 2–5 m bands. For each band, we sum the covered voxel counts across frames and divide by the summed eligible voxel counts. Retention uses the same calculation but evaluates each voxel only in frames after it was first correctly covered within 10 m of the robot. This measures whether mapped glass remains represented, separately from how quickly it is first detected. 

Spill. A predicted voxel is spill if it contains neither active GT glass nor an observed physical surface. This is the no-spill condition of (1), with St approximated by the surfaces actually observed during the run. Ground spill lies within 0.25 m of the estimated floor; all remaining spill is air spill. Spill is charged only while the robot is within 10 m of the false voxel (the placement range). D. Component Ablation 

Table V removes one component at a time from the full system. Ever coverage stays at about 82–88% across all configurations, so the components separate on false occupancy rather than on coverage. The angle gate contributes substantially on both measures. Without it, concurrent spill rises by 75% and 126% more distinct false voxels enter the map, on every scene ( +40% to 

+291% )—a larger accumulated rise than removing the entire manager ( +78% ), while Ever coverage differs by 0.9 points, well within the scene-to-scene spread. Multi-view verification shows the clearest tradeoff, and its benefit is not uniform across metrics. Enabling it reduces accumulated spill by 10% and raises Ever coverage by 3.2

points, but lowers near-field current coverage by 5.4 points and slightly increases concurrent spill, from 18 .7 to 19 .4 voxels per frame. The concurrent coverage it costs outweighs the spill it removes, which makes this a genuine tradeoff rather than a free gain. Yet planes placed at an incorrect depth that do not touch the ground need this mechanism to be removed; no other component reaches them, so a user should weigh it for their application. Nevertheless, the gaps it opens are short: measured on GG-360 across the nine scenes of Table III at ρ = 1 .0 m, their median length is 2–5 frames and 83 –94% are recovered. The mechanisms are complementary. Removing merging, floor evidence, and multi-view verification one at a time changes concurrent spill by +74% , +33% , and −4% , about 

+104% in total, whereas removing them together raises it by 

352% . When one mechanism is absent the others catch much of what it would have removed. Even without global management, GG-pin covers far more glass than either baseline under the same pinhole input and insert-only policy ( 88 .2% Ever against 48 .0% and 51 .8% ). Its concurrent false occupancy is comparable to GlassRecon (87 .7 versus 79 .2 voxels per frame) and less than one third of MonoGlass3D’s, while it admits fewer distinct false voxels over the run than either ( 478 versus 585 and 2059 ). The advantage over prior work therefore does not rest on global map management alone. 

E. Qualitative Analysis 

Figure 4 compares glass mapping and its effect on local planning across all methods and environment types. Without glass detection, planned paths cross physical glass boundaries; both GG variants recover the barriers while largely preserving traversable space, whereas the baselines’ false occupancy extends into traversable regions and, under severe map con-tamination, suppresses or shortens the displayed local paths. These examples illustrate the planning relevance of jointly reducing missed glass and spill into free space; they demon-strate planner integration but are not treated as a quantitative navigation-success evaluation. The accompanying video shows these runs in full. VI. C ONCLUSION 

In this work, we present GlassGuard, a navigation-oriented framework for reliable architectural-glass mapping in real-world environments. We approach the problem from a new angle, from how we formulate the objective, to depth-free 2D geometric verification, to persistent system-level verification from multiple sources of global evidence. Extensive real-world experiments across diverse environments demonstrate higher glass coverage with substantially less false occupancy than the evaluated baselines. Room for improvement remains: faster detection and inference would raise the update rate, and the current planar, near-vertical assumption could be extended to curved or strongly tilted glass, which we leave to future work.
