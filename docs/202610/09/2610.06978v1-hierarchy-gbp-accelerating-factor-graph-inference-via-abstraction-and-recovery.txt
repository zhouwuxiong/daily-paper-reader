## ABSTRACT 

Gaussian Belief Propagation (GBP) is a distributed inference algorithm that passes messages in graphical models, making it attractive for scalable spatial intelligence. However, we find GBP most effective locally: it rapidly smooths message errors that vary sharply between neighbor variables, but corrects global errors across distant graph regions incrementally through long-range message propagations. We propose Hierarchy-GBP (H-GBP) , an iterative, two-stage framework that acceler-ates GBP by first solving these global errors with a coarse graph approximation (abstraction) and projecting the results back to the original graph (recovery), then refining the remaining local errors with GBP. We prove H-GBP convergence to the optimum by deriving the combined matrix operator of our abstraction and recovery steps and analyzing its spectral radius. Experiments on linear sparse graphs show that H-GBP converges fundamentally faster than standard GBP. Moreover, we validate H-GBP on two important spatial problems: Pose Graph Optimization (PGO) and Bundle Adjustment (BA). H-GBP markedly accelerates large-scale PGO and achieves state-of-the-art runtime across all tested BA scales. GBP

## 1 INTRODUCTION 

Gaussian Belief Propagation (GBP) is an efficient distributed graphical inference algorithm (Pearl, 2022; Yates et al., 2026) that has been successfully applied in error-correcting decoding (Frey & MacKay, 1997), multiuser detection (Bickson et al., 2008), and solving large-scale linear systems (Shental et al., 2008). Inference proceeds by passing Gaussian distributions between nodes in a probabilistic graphical model, iteratively updating each variable’s marginal belief. This local, decoupled, and scalable computational model makes GBP a highly promising inference and reasoning framework for future spatial intelligence systems (Davison & Ortiz, 2019).

GBP has the desirable property that, if it converges on a Gaussian graph, it converges to the global optimal mean (Weiss & Freeman, 1999). However, its convergence rate is compromised by graph structures like local loops or long graph diameters (Malioutov et al., 2005; 2006; Du et al., 2018), and GBP can often require many iterations (El-Kurdi et al., 2012; Liu et al., 2012). Algebraically, GBP behaves as a smoother: it quickly damps local (high-frequency) errors that vary sharply between neighboring nodes, while global (low-frequency) errors across distant graph regions require many iterations to reduce. We observe the local-versus-global error separation in many spatial inference problems, including SLAM, 3D computer vision, and sensor networks. This motivates a two-level view of GBP (see Fig.1). The fine space is the original variable space where GBP performs local message passing, while the coarse space is a low-dimensional graph approximation suitable for removing low-frequency errors. We solidify this idea with a localized low-rank abstraction . Variables are grouped locally and abstracted via their most prominent eigenvalues, forming a coarse problem that captures low-frequency errors (Briggs et al., 2000; Nataf et al., 2011; Spillane et al., 2014). Our algorithm Hierarchy-GBP (H-GBP) alternates coarse residual correction with fine-level syn-chronous GBP. For linear Gaussian factor graphs, we derive an operator describing a ‘V-cycle’ of alternating coarse and fine steps, which lets us compute the spectral radius and predict a large im-provement in convergence rate compared to standard GBP on the fine graph. Experiments on sparse spatial factor graphs (Dellaert & Kaess, 2017; Davison, 2018; Papadimitriou, 2025; Jiao et al., 2026) verify this predicted acceleration in linear problems. We also show that the abstraction-and-recovery principle extends to practically important, non-linear Pose Graph Optimization (PGO) (see Fig.2) and Bundle Adjustment (BA) (see Fig.3). Specifically, our main contributions are: • H-GBP framework We introduce a multilevel GBP framework for general sparse factor graphs. H-GBP builds a low-rank coarse space from low-eigenvalue modes, and alternates coarse corrections with GBP updates on the original fine graph. • Empirical validation H-GBP converges faster than standard GBP on sparse linear factor graphs empirically. Extensive experiments on PGO and BA benchmarks show H-GBP’s speed and accuracy benefits, and demonstrate the potential for broader spatial intelligence applications. • Convergence theory For linear factor graphs, we establish a global precision convergence rate and derive a reduced cycle operator whose spectral radius is a geometric global conver-gence criterion. We prove the local convergence of H-GBP on manifolds. 22 RELATED WORK 

GBP on linear-Gaussian graphs has a strong theoretical foundation. Weiss & Freeman (1999) shows that when loopy GBP converges on a Gaussian graphical model, it recovers the correct posterior means, though the variances can be overconfident. Subsequent work connected GBP to walk-sum expansions and linear/quadratic solvers, clarifying that its behavior is governed by global coupling structure rather than by any single local loop in isolation (Malioutov et al., 2006; Shental et al., 2008; Moallemi & Van Roy, 2009). More recent analyses extended these perspectives to vector-valued Gaussian models, which arise in estimation problems (Du et al., 2018; Yates et al., 2026). Our H-GBP analysis takes a matrix-operator view of the underlying dynamics. A second line of work improves GBP efficiency while remaining on a flat graph. Representative ideas include residual scheduling (Elidan et al., 2006), relaxation or damping (El-Kurdi et al., 2012), and structural modifications such as feedback message passing (Liu et al., 2012). These methods can reduce oscillation or avoid unnecessary updates, but they do so by reordering or partially restructuring message passing on the original graph. They do not build a coarse representation to divide and counter long-range, low-frequency error components. H-GBP is inspired by domain-decomposition methods, where local smoothers remove high-frequency errors and coarse spaces correct slowly varying components (Briggs et al., 2000). The closest prior work combines GBP with multigrid for PDE/FEM systems (El-Kurdi et al., 2014; 2015). In contrast, H-GBP constructs the hierarchy directly on sparse factor graphs and derives the coupled coarse/fine message dynamics. To our knowledge, it is the first multilevel GBP framework formulated and analyzed for general sparse factor graphs beyond PDE/FEM discretizations. Our work is also related to the factor-graph optimization literature in robotics (Tazaki, 2022). Factor graphs have become a standard abstraction for large-scale estimation and SLAM, while sparse nonlinear solvers such as g2o and Ceres remain the dominant practical baselines (Dellaert & Kaess, 2017; Kümmerle et al., 2011; Agarwal et al., 2023). Recently, there has been renewed interest in GBP as a distributed inference engine for spatial estimation, including multi-robot localization, continuous-time SLAM, and bundle adjustment on graph processors (Murai et al., 2024; Hug et al., 2024; Ortiz et al., 2020). H-GBP sits between these threads: it preserves the locality and parallelism of GBP with an abstract-then-recover acceleration mechanism.

H-GBP naturally extends to nonlinear factor graph optimization by operating on linearizations in the tangent space. We use the standard right-perturbation convention from Lie theory (Solà et al., 2018) and factor-graph optimization (Dellaert & Kaess, 2017): at the current estimate ¯X, each residual admits the first-order approximation rf ( ¯Xf ⊕ ξf ) ≈ rf ( ¯Xf ) + Jf ξf . This yields the linear Gaussian system in the tangent space that we solve using H-GBP, before retracting ¯Xi ← ¯Xi Exp( ξi).Optimization on manifolds involves repeating the above process continuously until ¯Xi converges.

## 7 CONCLUSION 

We introduced Hierarchy-GBP, an abstraction-and-recovery framework to accelerate GBP on factor graphs by combining local message passing with a low-rank coarse correction. We showed H-GBP’s convergence by analyzing the spectral radius of a derived matrix operator for one cycle of linear H-GBP. We found H-GBP is competitive in both runtime and solution quality on challenging pose-graph and bundle adjustment benchmarks. The results highlight H-GBP as a promising design principle for scalable spatial-intelligence systems. D.1 IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 

All timings are CPU-only, on an Intel Core i9-13900HX system (24 physical cores, 32 logical processors, 64 GB RAM). A 16-thread run specifies a worker budget, not 16 physical cores. The released configurations specify one policy for SE(2), one for SE(3), and one for BA; none selects parameters by dataset name. 

Precision reuse and packed messages. Within a fixed Gaussian model, precision updates are independent of the canonical vectors. Once the precision stopping rule is met, we cache the Schur gain 

Bf →i = Λ fij (Λ fjj + Λ j\f )−1, η+  

> f→i

= ηfi − Bf →i(ηfj + ηj\f ).

Subsequent eta-only sweeps reuse both this gain and the belief precisions, replacing repeated small factorizations by matrix–vector products. This changes the per-message arithmetic from O(d3) to 

O(d2) for block dimension d. Caches are refreshed when the linearized model changes. Numerical freezing uses the task-specific tolerances below, rather than assuming an exact fixed point after a prescribed number of sweeps. 

Local basis construction. For PGO, a group G assembles HG ∈ Rd|G|× d|G| from its internal factors and priors, and retains the rG lowest eigenmodes as PG. Here d = 3 for SE(2) and d = 6 for SE(3), with ranks 4 and 12 , respectively (capped by the group dimension). SE(2) uses block inverse iteration with Rayleigh–Ritz extraction and an eigen-residual acceptance test, warm-started from the preceding basis when available. SE(3) first exploits exact band structure with a selected-eigenpair solver when the local block is sufficiently narrow-banded, and otherwise attempts the partial solve. Unaccepted partial solves fall back to a full symmetric eigendecomposition. These backends target the same low-mode space; local bases are rebuilt at each relinearization. 

Parallel and distributed structure. Fine-level factor and belief passes, as well as per-group basis construction, admit parallel execution with local data access. Contiguous message slots, fixed-size block kernels, preallocated workspaces, and persistent worker regions reduce allocation and synchronization overhead; restriction accumulates group contributions and prolongation applies local basis blocks. The one-thread PGO path avoids launching parallel worker teams. Our implementation is shared-memory: reductions and the reduced solve still provide global coupling. In preliminary experiments, GBP on the induced second-level factor graph was slower than a direct solve. A likely reason is the much smaller coarse-system dimension, which makes a factorization reused across corrections cheaper than repeated coarse message sweeps. We therefore use sparse Cholesky for the PGO coarse system and dense LDL ⊤ for the small BA coarse system, while retaining loopy GBP on the fine message graph. D.2 LINEAR EXPERIMENT DETAILS

Toward multiple levels. Algorithmically, extending the current two-level construction to a fully multilevel hierarchy is nontrivial. The upper-level system 

Acδz = rc

is a Galerkin algebraic system, and different coarse factor-graph realizations may induce the same matrix equation. Thus a multilevel GBP construction must decide not only the dimension of each coarse variable, but also how unary and binary factors should be assigned among abstracted coarse nodes. A natural attempt is to assign one coarse variable to each fine group, with dimension equal to the retained local eigenbasis size, project within-group factors into unary coarse factors, and project cross-group factors into between-group coarse factors. In our preliminary experiments, however, GBP on this induced coarse factor graph typically required many iterations to solve the coarse system and was slower than a direct sparse solve at that level. This does not diminish the two-level result; rather, it highlights a compelling open direction: designing purely message-passing upper levels that retain the scalability and locality needed for fully distributed spatial-intelligence systems. 

Broader applications. Finally, the abstraction-and-recovery principle behind H-GBP is not specific to pose graphs or bundle adjustment’s cameras. Many sparse factor-graph problems have natural intermediate structures that could define useful coarse abstractions. In multi-robot SLAM and navigation, each robot’s local subgraph could form a group, with higher-level messages coordinating inter-robot constraints. In visual odometry, pixels, patches, or object-level regions could be grouped into progressively coarser variables to accelerate GBP-based inference. Exploring such applications may reveal when hierarchical message passing can provide both distributed computation and strong global correction in broader sparse graphical models. 33
